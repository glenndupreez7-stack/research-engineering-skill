# Research and evidence

Use this module for technical investigations, fact checking, comparisons, and research-backed recommendations.

## Investigation loop

1. **Define the answer target.** State the decision or question, scope, relevant time period, product/version, comparison baseline, and what would count as sufficient evidence.
2. **Discover broadly, then narrow.** Use broad searches to learn terminology and locate candidate sources. Open the actual source pages or files; snippets and generated summaries are leads, not evidence. Follow official references and citations where they materially answer the question.
3. **Rank sources for the claim.** Prefer the authority that owns or directly establishes the fact: specifications, official documentation, first-party APIs, source code, release notes, datasets, or original research. Use reputable secondary sources for orientation, interpretation, and leads. A source's authority does not guarantee freshness or completeness; check version, date, scope, and definitions.
4. **Capture a compact evidence record.** For each material claim, note the source title/publisher, direct URL, relevant section or passage, access date, applicable version/date, what it supports, and status (verified, inferred, conflicting, or not checked). Save raw output only when needed for a multi-step, collaborative, or auditable task; keep it in a scoped artifact with sensitive data removed.
5. **Check meaning and comparability.** Confirm units, denominator, timeframe, population, configuration, definitions, and comparison baseline for numerical or benchmark claims. For code behavior, compare documentation with the actual version and observable implementation.
6. **Test material conflicts.** Seek evidence that could disconfirm the leading explanation. Resolve contradictions with targeted follow-up research. Explain which source governs and why; if the conflict remains material, present both interpretations and the consequence.
7. **Synthesize after gathering evidence.** Separate verified facts from observations, inferences, assumptions, recommendations, and open questions. Use calibrated language and qualify claims beside the affected statement.
8. **Stop when sufficient.** Stop when the question is answered to the stated standard and material contradictions are resolved or explicitly bounded. Do not keep searching solely for reassurance.

## Citation and provenance

- Cite factual claims, numbers, dates, API behavior, and non-obvious technical recommendations near the text they support.
- Prefer a direct, stable URL to the specific document, section, source file, or release. Include a title and version/date when links alone do not identify the evidence.
- Make clear whether a claim is supported directly or is an inference. A citation to a broad homepage is not a substitute for the relevant evidence.
- For changing documentation, record the version, release, branch, or access date as appropriate. Recheck mutable sources before reusing old findings for a consequential decision.
- Attribute quotations exactly and quote only what is needed. Paraphrase in original language and do not overstate what a source establishes.
- If evidence is inaccessible, indirect, incomplete, or contradictory, say so; do not manufacture a citation or imply verification.

## Research deliverables

Choose a format that serves the question. A useful default is: direct answer; key supported findings; implications or recommendation (if requested); uncertainties; and references. Avoid adding a generic methodology section unless the user wants one or it materially helps evaluate the result. Keep research files discoverable within the project and state their location when useful.
